The formula *epea pteroenta* serves two functions within the poem. On a purely imagistic level, the phrase evokes the swiftness, irrevocability, directness, and reciprocal nature of speech—the adjective affirms the basic notion in the noun. On the narrative level, as a synonymous phrase for *muthos*, this formulaic line can be used to introduce highly marked speeches. Only sixty-one of the poem's approximately six hundred speeches are described as "winged words," and this selectivity has real meaning (Martin 1993:30-31).

These sixty-one speeches share a common pragmatic speech situation: winged words are spoken by one person to one other—rarely to two—and the addressee is in close contact with the speaker, usually as a comrade-in-arms. More significantly, every speech called "winged words" constitutes one specific speech-act class: in the terminology of speech-act theory, all sixty-one are "directives"—every speech thus introduced is meant to make the listener do something. The underlying unity is not immediately apparent because diverse surface structures—direct imperatives, hints, statements of need, questions, narratives—can each perform the same illocutionary function in an appropriate context (Martin 1993:31-32).

Achilles' winged words upon recognizing Athena in Book 1 (1.201-5) illustrate this at once: the speech takes the form of an interrogative—"Why have you come, child of aegis-bearing Zeus? Is it to see the *hubris* of Agamemnon, son of Atreus?"—yet functions as a directive: Athena should perceive things the way Achilles does. Her reply confirms that she fully reads the illocutionary force of his words rather than responding as if they constituted an actual request for information. Because form and function can vary in the employment of directives, the individual speaker has opportunity to perform and to exercise expressive creativity (Martin 1993:32-33).

The formula ἔν τ' ἄρα οἱ φῦ χειρί, ἔπος τ' ἔφατ' ἔκ τ' ὀνόμαζεν—"she put her hand on him and spoke a word and called"—and its variant χειρί τέ μιν κατέρεξεν ἔπος τ' ἔφατ' ἔκ τ' ὀνόμαζεν introduce motherly, comforting language: Thetis to Achilles (1.361, 19.7, 24.127), Dione to Aphrodite (5.372), Hekabe to Hektor (6.253), or words between intimates such as Hektor and his wife (6.406, 485). The "hand-and-speech" lines represent a consistent poetic expression drawing the audience's attention to a single genre of speech: consolation (Martin 1993:18-19).

What is important in these formulas is the coordinated hand and speech gesture rather than any usefulness for introducing vocatives. Lines that include the "hand" phrase consistently introduce consolation; lines that share only the second segment—"spoke a word and called"—are employed far more freely, introducing speeches full of taunts and insult, simple questions, berating speech, concession, and invitations to pray. The lines sharing only the second segment seem to represent an innovation, a reuse of the original full-line formula without a sense of its original poetic purpose. *Epos* in the coordinated formula is conceived as a physical thing: gesture and utterance, the latter imagined to be just as physical as the former, claim notice (Martin 1993:19-20).

The heroic imperative as crystallized in Phoinix's words to Achilles—μύθων τε ῥητήρ' ἔμεναι πρηκτῆρά τε ἔργων (9.443)—demands that a hero learn to "be a doer of deeds" and a speaker, not of words, but of *muthoi*, "authoritative speech-acts." Both deeds and such words can be enacted (Martin 1993:25).

Diomedes' development within the *Iliad* illustrates the striving for this ideal. When Nestor approves the substance of his speech but tells him he has not reached the "perfection of speeches" (*telos muthōn*, 9.56), Nestor then demonstrates it himself—delivering a simple proposal in elegant form, with polyptoton, asyndetic gnomic statements, and full praise for his audience Agamemnon, all of whom approve his *muthon* (9.79). By Book 14, Diomedes has learned to construct an impregnable speech by preemptive action against possible objections, and the poet concludes his proposal with the identical line that followed Nestor's exemplary *muthos* in Book 9: "Thus he spoke and then they listened and obeyed" (14.133 = 9.79) (Martin 1993:24-25).

Aeneas' words to Achilles at 20.203-204—"We know each other's genealogy, we know the parents, from hearing the famous *epea* of mortal men"—illuminate the fundamental distinction between *muthos* and *epos*. The adjective *proklut'* literally means "heard before"; only in the context of oral tradition can this word come to mean "famous," as happens also with the noun *kleos*, "glory." Gregory Nagy has shown that *epea* here refers to poetic utterances in the form of traditional narratives about Aeneas. What the passage underlines is the significant distinction between "telling" blameful things (*oneidea*)—an act described with the verb *muthēsasthai*—and "hearing" utterances (*epea*): *muthos* is associated with the speaker's action in giving a message, while *epos* refers to the transmission of the message, the end-product of the speech process (Martin 1993:16-17).

In the same speech at 20.246-50, Aeneas develops the contrast further: *muthoi* are figured as heavy, tangible goods—"nor could a ship with a hundred benches bear the load"—while *epea* "travel about here and there in a wide field," like small objects that can be batted to and fro, part of a general system of exchange: "whatever *epos* you say, you hear in return." *Muthoi* are the large units, *epea* the small (Martin 1993:17-18).

@urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0012.tlg001:4.337-4.340
:citation_urn: urn:cts:CHS:Commentaries.AHCIP:Iliad.4.337-4.340
:created_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z
:updated_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z

The poet introduces Agamemnon's speech to Menestheus and Odysseus in the troop-rousing scene as "winged words" (4.337). The speech employs the interrogative form—"Why do you stand off cowering and wait for the rest?" (4.337-40)—but its directive force is amplified by the next words: "It is fitting for you two to stand with the front-lines and confront burning battle" (4.341-42), a statement that is just as "directive" in function although it too lacks the linguistic form of an imperative.

Odysseus responds to the speech by labeling it with a formulaic line (4.350): "Atreus' son, what sort of *epos* has escaped the fence of your teeth?" Yet at 4.357 the poet says "he took back the *muthos*." Rather than being a flaw in the system, this co-occurrence is the key to Homeric usage of speech terms: *epea* can be a periphrastic expression for *muthos*, but not vice versa (Martin 1993:29).

@urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0012.tlg001:5.715
:citation_urn: urn:cts:CHS:Commentaries.AHCIP:Iliad.5.715
:created_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z

At 5.715, Hera refers to a past commitment as a *muthos* with the qualifier *halion* ("in vain"): "Vain, indeed, is the *muthos* we promised Menelaos." Achilles uses the same adjective *halion* at 18.324 for a past speech-act but the line concludes with *epos* instead: "A vain *epos* I tossed forth that day." The nature of the original speech-act explains the difference: as a boast, Achilles' speech is attracted into the language of actual boast descriptions, which often contain the formula *eukhomenos epos ēuda*. External factors—formulaic pressure, poetic themes in the larger discourse—can mask the inherent semantic distinction between *muthos* and *epos* (Martin 1993:28).

@urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0012.tlg001:5.816-5.817
:citation_urn: urn:cts:CHS:Commentaries.AHCIP:Iliad.5.816-5.817
:created_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z
:updated_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z

At 5.816-17, Diomedes labels his talk with Athena an *epos*: "I recognize you, goddess, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus. Therefore I will candidly say a word (*epos*) and will not conceal it." Achilles similarly refers to Athena's private advice (1.216) and his own prayer to Zeus (16.236) with the word *epos*. Prayer, which epitomizes private communication, is in fact never designated *muthos* in the *Iliad*.

A further indication of the personal nature of *epos* comes from Homer's adjective usage: possessives frequently accompany the word *epos*—Achilles speaks of "your word" (1.216), Achilles praying to Zeus and Hera using a similar formula to Hypnos say "You have heard my word before" (*emon epos eklues*, 14.234, 16.236), and Zeus refers to his own command with this phrase (8.8). By contrast, *muthos* is rarely referred to by a speaker as his or her own or as that of his or her addressee (Martin 1993:38).

@urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0012.tlg001:7.284-7.286
:citation_urn: urn:cts:CHS:Commentaries.AHCIP:Iliad.7.284-7.286
:created_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z
:updated_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z

The duel of Hektor and Ajax would have continued if not for the intervention of the heralds, one of whom, the Trojan Idaios, "said a *muthos*" (*eipe te muthon*, 7.277). Ajax replies: "Bid Hektor to speak these things (*muthēsasthai*). For he himself challenged all the best to battle. Let him take the lead" (7.284-86). Hektor then pronounces the end of the duel (7.290-93): "Let us cease from battle and strife."

Because Homeric poetry so keenly attends to socially correct forms of speech, Hektor's words are more than a simple directive: they represent an actual "performative utterance"—language that brings about a desired end by virtue of the very pronouncement. There are "felicity conditions" for performative utterances, and the poem respects just such parameters: to be linguistically correct, Hektor, who began the duel with the challenge, must "pronounce" its end. Ajax, with a good sense of propriety that marks the most successful Homeric warriors, defers to his opponent. Idaios has an exact analogue in the religious or legal official who instructs the groom to say "I thee wed." (Martin 1993:40).

@urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0012.tlg001:7.375
:citation_urn: urn:cts:CHS:Commentaries.AHCIP:Iliad.7.375
:created_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z
:updated_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z

The adjective *puknos* and its by-form *pukinos*, "dense" or "tightly-packed," are the only words associated with both *epos* and *pteroeis* when those are examined separately; when applied to speech, the adjective carries the sense of language that is dense with meaning and filled with urgency. Priam's formal bid to call a truce (7.375) is the first of four instances of *pukinon epos* in the *Iliad* (7.375, 11.788, 24.75, 24.744).

The adjective *pukinon* modifies arms and armor, beds, troop formations, house construction, branches, and clouds; mental products can also be thus qualified—a tightly constructed plan (2.55), Odyssean wiles (3.202), an ambush (4.392), a trick (6.187). This particularized form of speech is the paradigm for the best kind of *epos*, speech that has become a lasting possession for its hearer, the "last word" to remain with an intimate (Achilles, Andromakhe) or to put an end to strife (the truce in Book 7, the ransom in Book 24) (Martin 1993:35).

By Book 14, Diomedes has learned how to construct an impregnable speech by taking preemptive action against possible objections. As does Nestor, he prefaces his speech with disarming recollection of the past—his own ancestry in this case—and thus rejects possible claims that he is too young to speak well, while raising his status as a warrior by blood. As a result, his *muthos* is assured of success: "Therefore, you cannot devalue my *muthos* by saying I am strengthless and have a bad ancestry, whatever I say" (14.126-27). @urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0012.tlg001:8.5-8.27
:citation_urn: urn:cts:CHS:Commentaries.AHCIP:Iliad.8.5-8.27
:created_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z
:updated_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z

The speech that Zeus addresses to all the Olympians at the beginning of Book 8 represents the ideal of the genre of commands (Martin 1993:54). It shares with all *muthoi* of command a concentration on the act to be performed by words. As with other *muthoi*, the speech thus labeled is subject to public scrutiny before an audience—it is a performance in a speech-act sense as well as in a wider social-poetic sense. As do other *muthoi*, it reaches for length and elaboration as an emblem of authoritative, important communication, and asserts the status of the speaker.

Zeus' speech outdoes other *muthoi* by exaggeration. The rhetorical distancing he accomplishes at 8.5-27 finds expression in a powerful image chosen to boast of his status: threatening to hurl any who disobey into Tartaros, he backs up his words by picturing the massed Olympians tugging at him by a golden chain. Though he might yank them up, together with earth and sea, the gods could not pull him down. In turning the horizontal line of communication among presumed equals into a vertical chain, Zeus enacts the gods' dependence on him and dramatizes his own rhetorical ability—the power of making convincing images—at the same time that he solidifies his political position. He is a master at the poetics of power. The *muthos* of what "might" happen is actually a projection of the current power configuration on Olympus, and functions like "myth" in the wider sense as a politically important act of symbolic discourse (Martin 1993:54-55).

It is characteristic of the greatest speakers in the poem that their *muthoi* often have a self-referential focus on the very act of speaking. Zeus prohibits the gods from supporting either side in the war but phrases this prohibition in terms of speech: rivals wish to "cut" his utterance (*diakersai emon epos*), as if it resembled the chain he mentions later. Along with this prohibition, Zeus introduces a positive injunction: "But all together praise (*aineit'*), so that I complete these deeds as quickly as possible" (8.7-9). The critical need for the approval of his Olympian audience—the "praise" alluded to at 8.9—shows how an oral culture's notions of performance structure the distribution of power. Only an acceptable "performance" of a proposal enables the speaker to accomplish his will; only a counter-performance, the actual voicing of "praise," certifies the audience's consent (Martin 1993:55-56).

The formulaic line "Act, but we other gods will not all approve" (*epaineomen*) occurs three times (4.29, 16.443, 22.181), each time marking the moments when Zeus has just suggested that a hero's life be saved. On two of these occasions, Hera identifies Zeus' proposal as a *muthos*, prefacing her reply with the formula, "O most dread son of Kronos, what sort of *muthos* have you said?" (4.25 = 16.440). The threat of implied public blame among the gods seems to force Zeus to yield, yet he appears prepared for this outcome. His *muthoi* that seek approval for intervention are in complete contrast with the *muthos* of Book 8 that prohibits interference—there he uses the rhetoric of force, but when plotting something contrary to the will of Athena and Hera, he portrays himself as undecided, adjusting his rhetoric accordingly (Martin 1993:56-57).

@urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0012.tlg001:4.336-4.421
:citation_urn: urn:cts:CHS:Commentaries.AHCIP:Iliad.4.336-4.421
:created_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z
:updated_at: 2026-06-11T00:00:00.000Z

Book 4 of the poem is a good place to begin observing the poetics of flyting. The key lines for identifying this activity as part of the authoritative speech of *muthos* are 4.356-57: "Smiling at him Agamemnon addressed him, as he knew of his anger, and took back the *muthos*." The speech thus referred to on its retraction had been labeled at 4.336 with the verb *neikessen*. This co-occurrence confirms that each speech Agamemnon makes in the episode is an instantiation of *muthos* discourse. Twice, further (4.337, 4.369), the introduction to Agamemnon's speeches couples the verb *neikō* with the formula "he spoke winged words," also a regular introduction to directives—further confirmation that flyting speeches represent a form of *muthos* (Martin 1993:68-69).

Not only is the diction of the speech introductions standardized; Agamemnon's rhetorical tacks also follow a pattern. He begins his three *neikos* speeches (as distinct from his "gentle words" to Idomeneus, the Ajaxes, and Nestor at 4.256, 285, and 313) with questions: "Argives, disgraces, are you not ashamed?" (4.242); "Why do you stand off shrinking in fear?" (4.340); "Why do you cower, why do you steal glances at the banks of war?" (4.371). Next, he compares his addressees to scared animals. His general rebuke to the Argives pictures them as fawns halted by fear (4.243-45); to Odysseus and Diomedes he uses the verb *kataptōssō* (4.340, 4.371), related to the word for "rabbit." The final strategy relies on privileging another place, implying that the addressee occupies a position of no importance: to the Argives, engagement in battle is contrasted with waiting by the ships; Odysseus and Menestheus are told they prefer the *dais* to battle (4.343-46); Diomedes is provoked by mention of his father Tydeus' exploits at Thebes (Martin 1993:69-70).

It is not coincidental that Agamemnon selects Odysseus and Diomedes as targets: these two heroes pose the greatest threat, next to Achilles, in their verbal abilities. Odysseus deftly parries the accusation of laxness with a rhetorical question, then shifts to speak of the future: "You will see the father of Telemakhos mixing in the front lines" (4.354). Finally, he criticizes Agamemnon's style of speech: "You are talking idly" (*anemōlia bazeis*, 4.355). Agamemnon's response certifies that Odysseus defeated him verbally: he takes back the *neikos* speech, denying that he was even attempting to blame Odysseus (4.359), and adopts Odysseus' own poetic formulation to waft away his previous speech: "May the gods make all these things like the winds" (*metamōnia*—cf. *anemōlia* earlier, both from the noun *anemos*, "wind") (Martin 1993:70-71).

If Odysseus resists Agamemnon by clever riposte, Diomedes' strategy is cunning silence. Agamemnon has baited a trap: "Such was Tydeus. But the son he begat is worse in war—better in speaking" (4.399-400). Under these terms, were Diomedes to attempt an Odyssean reply, he would simply affirm the accusation that he is a better talker than fighter. The "silent" answer works because it constitutes an ambiguous sign. The rules of the flyting genre allow of two interpretations: silence can be read as incompetence in the genre of insults, or as a strategic aloofness asserting social superiority over the abuser. Diomedes knows the rules well enough to rebuke his companion Sthenelos, who has tried to counteract Agamemnon's insults (4.404-10): by directing his reply to Sthenelos and getting him to consent to a *muthos* of command (4.412), Diomedes acknowledges that he knows the ambiguous import of his silence in the duel (Martin 1993:71).

@urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0012.tlg001:1.259-1.274
:citation_urn: urn:cts:CHS:Commentaries.AHCIP:Iliad.1.259-1.274

In his first intervention in the poem, Nestor uses the device of recalling the past in order to legitimate his claim on authority in the present: "But obey. You are both younger than I. Already I have been with men better than you and they never slighted me" (1.259-74). The act of recalling here focuses precisely on Nestor's ability to command: "And they understood my counsels and obeyed the *muthos*" (1.273). The ability to command—mastery of the first *muthos* genre—is enhanced when the speaker has the ability to foreground his own directives by retrojecting the act of command through another rhetorical genre, recollection. This latter genre, apart from commands and flyting, is the only other one to be labeled *muthos* (Martin 1993:79-80).

The master of the genre of memory is Nestor. What the *Iliad* seems to leave as a gap—having been shown the persuasive power of Nestor, based on his ability to remember and remind, an audience might expect him to be the one capable of convincing Achilles to rejoin the battle—is resolved by the fact that Odysseus takes on the job, and fails; Nestor succeeds, but with the wrong audience, Patroklos. The central trope in both these speeches is explicitly an act of memory. Odysseus says of Peleus' speech: "Thus the old man instructed, but you forget" (9.259). The same line recurs in Nestor's words to Patroklos (11.790). Nor is the strategy of dramatic memory limited to these two speakers: Phoinix, who stands in for Nestor in the embassy scene, uses a long discourse from memory as the centerpiece of his attempt to induce Achilles to return (9.527) (Martin 1993:80-81).

As a general rule, characters in the *Iliad* do not remember anything simply for the pleasure of memory. Recall has an exterior goal. Memory bestows a mantle on its practitioners: in recalling the words of Kalkhas, for example, Odysseus exhibits a seerlike ability to describe and interpret what is not present (Martin 1993:81). The crucial difference between the memory performance of Odysseus and that of Nestor has to do with the self-presentation of the speaker. Odysseus foregrounds himself as performer, explicitly quoting another authority whom he can be seen to overcome in the contest of speaking. Nestor, on the other hand, presents himself not as a speaker but as a heroic performer of both words and deeds. His narrative of personal experience is convincing because it calls for emulation, even challenge. It becomes the mainspring of memory that powers the entire second half of the poem's action (Martin 1993:82).

The address that Hekabe makes to Priam as he leaves for Achilles' tent is explicitly presented in the language of lament ("she wailed [*kōkusen*], and answered with a *muthos*," 24.200). The speech starts with desperate rebukes of Priam's folly in going—a tone comparable to Andromakhe's lament at 24.743-45, chiding Hektor for not consoling her. Even more explicit is the call for others to join in her mourning, a theme found in Greek lament tradition. Hekabe concludes with a dramatization of her anger and grief: she could eat the liver of Achilles; only such violence would offer requital. In sum, the speech laments both her son, now dead, and her husband, whom she does not expect to see alive again (Martin 1993:87-88).

Given the male, heroic in-group orientation of the word *muthos* and its association with powerful self-presentation, it would seem to be a social taboo for women to employ this kind of speech. But both Hekabe and Helen, who also addresses Priam with a *muthos* at 3.166-80, are in fact enacting laments in the speeches labeled with this word. They fulfill an expected performance role, using a recognized genre of *muthos* as memory, but they are presented as doing so at unexpected times, to create dramatic effect (Martin 1993:87).

Helen's use is equally marked. When Priam on the wall asks her to name a hero below (3.166), she delays this identification and in her reply uses the language of lament, recalling her former home and wishing she had died when she followed Paris to Troy (3.171-80). The poet's use of the line *Τὸν δ' Ελένη μύθοισιν ἀμείβετο, δῖα γυναικῶν* keys an audience by the use of the word *muthos* to a graceful enactment of desire in the context of lament (Martin 1993:88).

These lamentations by Helen and Hekabe fulfill the conditions for performance found throughout the analysis of the three *muthos* genres: they are acts of self-presentation with an emphasis on extension and detail, in a public setting. It is, as well, the genre of lament in the context of larger memories that finally unites Achilles in thought with Priam and effects the closure of the *Iliad*, both men remembering their losses (Martin 1993:87).

Athena's command to Achilles at 1.207-18 appears to be less the unfettered directive of a beneficent goddess and more a bargain struck among equals. By throwing this familiar encouragement at Hektor, Achilles violates a linguistic constraint with precisely the same tonal effect as when he uses "winged words" to an enemy. The ultimate rhetorical insult to a warrior is to be infantilized or feminized: if Achilles can "remind" Hektor how to fight, he has already negated Hektor's ability to win. This is a strategy Hektor knows as well, since he used a direct "quotation" to Patroklos (the alleged "order" of Achilles, 16.838-41) in order to reduce his adversary to the status of an unthinking, obedient follower of directions. Here the rhetoric of recollection comes full circle: a commander's persuasive power depends on an ability to construct memories, and so does a warrior's attack on the enemy (Martin 1993:83-85).

The speeches that Homer gives his characters further the narrative strategy of ranking heroic worth, as can be seen in the *teikhoskopia* in Book 3, a device designed to turn the narrator's exposition of mundane fact into a dramatically persuasive scene. At the same time, the speeches embody an assumption, often overlooked: that heroes themselves can evaluate one another's "style," particularly in the act of speaking. Along with characterizing Agamemnon and the others in terms of physique and prowess, this scene serves to show us speech styles, through the reminiscences of Antenor. The details he notices when recalling an earlier visit to Troy by Menelaos and Odysseus relate specifically to the "performance" of *muthoi* (3.212-23): "But when they wove speeches (*muthoi*) and plans for all\... Menelaos discoursed in running fashion, speaking little, but very clearly (*ligeōs*), since he is not much with words (*polumuthos*) nor one to cast words about (*apharmatoepēs*)\... But when Odysseus much with wiles got up, he'd stand, he'd look down fixing his eyes on the ground\... keeping still, looking like a witless man. Yet when he let the great voice out of his chest, and the words (*epea*), like winter flakes of snow, that is when no other mortal could contend with Odysseus" (Martin 1993:95-96).

Antenor assumes that there is a code of behavior for speakers in such situations, a set of performance conventions: one should move the scepter to make a point, for instance. Odysseus employs an unconventional strategy for capturing his audience, a style that plays off the shared knowledge of conventions, and thereby foregrounds his rhetorical act. By creatively modifying traditional material, Odysseus brings about a memorable performance. He creates a contrast between his appearance and the reality of his oratorical power; he characterizes himself by a *muthos*, the perfect vehicle for self-presentation. Such stylistic awareness is a cultural, and not just a "sophisticated," poetic process (Martin 1993:96).

The heroes' concern with speech style springs from the need to differentiate oneself and establish status, an imperative found in ethnographic descriptions of competitive cultures. That the status of a person is rated before the hero's speech emerges from Diomedes' remark, after giving his genealogy in full, that the Achaeans cannot now devalue his performance (*muthon atimēsaite*, 14.127) by calling him strengthless and ignoble. Speech style, status, and ranking must be taken as related manifestations of the workings of the all-important concept of *timē* (Martin 1993:97-98).

Nestor of Pylos enters the *Iliad* just when it seems all speech must fail, and his very first effort at persuasion succeeds. In a system that judges style by results, Nestor holds a preeminent place; this characteristic marks all his speech in the poem. The speech he makes on arrival is highly inventive in attempting to appease three separate audiences: the two flyting heroes and the Achaeans in camp. Moreover, the syntax and discourse structure of the speech enact, at a poetic level, the notion of mutual support aimed at in Nestor's words. Binary structures abound, presenting a rhetorical model for two-sidedness (Martin 1993:101-2):

"A great sorrow has come to Akhaia / There might rejoice Priam / and Priam's children / Other Trojans might be glad / if they hear of these two fighting / Who excel at counsel / and at fighting / Both are younger than me / I once consorted with better than you\..."

Agamemnon's comment, that Nestor has spoken *kata moiran*, can be taken literally: the command to be reconciled is rigorously "proportionate" at the poetic level, down to the rhythm and structure of each verse, as it is at the rhetorical level, according praise and blame equally to Agamemnon and Achilles. Nestor resembles the perfect praise-poet in the ideology of the Indo-European tradition precisely because he enacts this equal distribution. The epithet "having sweet words," *hēduepēs*, with which he is introduced in the *Iliad* (1.248), has a long heritage in this tradition, and furthermore refers to divine speech within Greek archaic poetry (Martin 1993:102).

The application of gnomic precedents and the employment of recollection present two more features characteristic of Nestor in this speech and in all his performances. The two strategies, proverbs and recollections, are related, in that both require the formal presentation of "memory," one on the level of narrative, the other that of the phrase. And memory is active throughout Nestor's rhetoric at yet another level, that of praise: he is continually "memorializing" his audience, in a manner akin to the poet's. We find that the totality of his performances marks Nestor as a unique orator, a speaker whose rhetoric rests on eulogy. It is exactly his role as praise-poet—therefore, as one who practices the craft of the epic itself—that gains Nestor the most explicitly favorable depiction of any speaker in the poem (Martin 1993:102-9).

The first critical term to examine in the analysis of Thersites' speech is the insult Odysseus throws at him: *akritomuthos* (2.246). The admiring description of Menelaos' style, *ou polumuthos* (3.214), was correlated with a style that did not miss the mark by tossing words about aimlessly: *oud' apharmatoepēs* (3.215). When Thersites appears for the first time, the poet draws attention to qualities in his speech that directly oppose the good style of Menelaos: he speaks a great deal and he does not put his words in good order (*epea . . . akosma . . . polla*, 2.213). The implication that Thersites misses the mark of proportionate speech comes in Homer's observation that the blamer says whatever he thinks will get a laugh (2.215). In other words, he is just an entertainer, not a "performer" in the heroic sense (Martin 1993:109-10).

Thersites' style deserves no respect because he does not have the heroic martial performance record needed to back up his words: style for the hero is a total notion, a proportion of words and deeds. Because Thersites does not have a valid poetic memory for his own career, the audience of Achaean heroes and epic hearers cannot perform the critical judgment necessary to validate his *muthos*. It remains "indeterminate" or "undiscriminated." The verbal idea contained in the first part of the compound adjective *akritomuthos* is one of careful selection, what a commander must exercise to pick out the best troops (2.362, *krin'*), the opposite of massing things together—in a word, critical ability. The term, then, underscores a performance value: the capacity to judge one's own acts and be judged for them (Martin 1993:110-11).

In the other passage where *muthoi* are called "undiscriminated," the lack of connection between verbal behavior and martial performance is prominently foregrounded. Iris rebukes Priam (2.796-97): "Old man, to you unjudged words are always dear as once during peace. But war inescapable has arisen." "Unjudged *muthoi*" is a contradiction in terms; such words would be a masquerade of martial speech in the context of peace. Real *muthoi* require action (Martin 1993:110-11).

The evidence of meter reinforces the stylistic analysis. When Thersites' speech (2.225-42) is performed aloud, it contains massive correption—the reduction of long vowels and diphthongs from their usual metrical value as "heavy" syllables to "light," short values. A count of correptions per number of lines shows that Thersites' eighteen verses contain this feature ten times, a rate of 55 percent. By contrast, Nestor's correption rate in the speech of 1.254-84 is 30 percent (nine times in thirty lines). The average rate of correption for narrative verses in the poem is 20 percent; for speeches it is 40 percent. Nestor, "of sweet speech," sounds like Homer as we first hear him; Thersites is quite literally "without meter" in his performance, markedly more so than the average hero (Martin 1993:112-13).

The encounter between Glaukos and Diomedes in Book 6 is the turning point in Diomedes' education in performance, as that affects his speech to enemies (Martin 1993:126). Within the *Iliad* the process of learning a tradition and the growth of creative ability at performance is depicted through Diomedes. Had there been little other evidence, this depiction alone would encourage the imagination that the poet in such a medium composed as he performed. After this encounter, the hero delivers several quite good flyting attacks, against Dolon (10.369 and 10.446), Hektor (11.361), and Paris (11.384).

The demand to know Glaukos' genealogy makes sense in the context of flyting, since it gives the speaker a hold with which to throw his opponent. The implication of Diomedes' remark that Glaukos has not appeared before in "man-glorifying" battle is that Glaukos has no ability to be glorified; and the reference to the "boldness" of Glaukos has to be read with an eye to the negative connotations of "boldness" when a speaker's deeds do not authorize this. The apparent digression into the story of Dionysos furthers this strategy: scared women, a comforting mother-figure, a threatening male who engages in rough language—these figures in Diomedes' miniature dramatization of "myth" are in fact projections of the participants in the very *muthos* of flyting in which he is engaged. For Dionysos and the nurses, read Glaukos; for Lykourgos, Diomedes; and for Thetis, compare the women of Troy. Diomedes' myth, then, is a version of his victory, a wish fulfillment of the threatening physical encounter (Martin 1993:127-28).

Glaukos is not naive. His first strategy—to question the value of genealogy—contains the famous image of the "generations of leaves," but in the language of Homer, falling to the ground connotes the end of human as well as vegetal life: this is a coded threat. The invitation to learn Glaukos' genealogy from him also contains a veiled insult: the reply, "Many men know it" (6.151), implies that Diomedes, who professes not to, is unpracticed in feats of memory, an essential genre for the performing hero to perfect. Glaukos' tale of ancestry is thus not factual information but makes sense only in performance: it is a symbol in a game of dueling narratives, an act of self-presentation that attempts to wrest authority (Martin 1993:128-29).

Diomedes' second speech—how Oineus, his grandfather, played host to Bellerophon (6.215-31)—is the only passage in which such a "myth" of Oineus' meeting with Bellerophon is ever mentioned because Diomedes has just invented the "tradition" in order to force Glaukos into the socially correct ritual exchange. He speaks to win, and does. Zeus "took away the wits of Glaukos, who exchanged golden armor for bronze, 100-cows'-worth for that of nine" (6.234-36): Glaukos resembles Laomedon, involved as he is in a shamefully bad exchange; Diomedes is analogous to Sarpedon, since he induced his enemy to switch armor. Achilles' flyting speeches are distinctive through their use of recollection, and his memory feats are uniquely antagonistic. What remain separable modes of speaking for other heroes are for him two sides of the same experience (Martin 1993:141). The strategy of rebuking by recollection appears clearly in his third reply to the embassy. Although Ajax has spoken "according to your heart" (*kata thumon*, 9.645), Achilles' heart still swells with anger; he expresses this in terms of memory: "Whenever I recall how the son of Atreus degraded me among the Argives, like some worthless itinerant" (9.646-47; Martin 1993:141-42).

The same technique appears in his speech to Thetis in Book 1, which recaps in miniature narrative the first episode in the poem with Achilles' slight reshaping. Only at two points does Achilles intrude as narrator: when he sums up Agamemnon's *muthos* as a "threat" (*ēpeilēsen*, 1.388); and when he concludes with the wish that Agamemnon may realize "his own destructive act, that he did not honor the best of the Achaeans" (1.411-12). The narrative is indirectly blaming Agamemnon; its framework is the discourse of flyting. But it is a more convincing rebuke precisely because Achilles restrains himself from indulging in name-calling throughout the narrative (Martin 1993:141).

Even more striking is what Achilles does not remember. His words at 16.72-73, describing the Trojans' flight "should Agamemnon act mildly towards me," do not acknowledge the embassy scene that has just occurred, and this has long puzzled readers. Achilles, whose entire style relies on recollection, who is shown recalling the exact words of his own men's complaints in this same book (16.203-6), surely remembers. By refusing to acknowledge the existence of Agamemnon's offer, Achilles engages in a quite recognizable Iliadic convention—recall the effective silent reply of Diomedes to Agamemnon (4.401). Achilles has played the poetic equivalent of *damnatio memoriae*. Thus, in the terms of heroic style, he has effectively outdone the rhetoric of Agamemnon, whose excessive style could only cause competitors to enter an escalating spiral if they choose to speak against him. A "successful act of revenge is one that so appropriately caps the original injury that it draws attention to its own significance" in a society that values heroic style. Achilles' great speech in Book 9 is as effective a reply as he could give, verbally; this "forgetful" silence in Book 16, however, is an even more damning statement (Martin 1993:142-43).

It is in the discourse of recollection that Hektor shows the clearest differences with other heroic speakers. A remarkable trait of his style is the use of direct quotation to dramatize for his audience what he imagines will happen. More than this, the quotations are actually representations of what already has taken place. In other words, Hektor displaces memory onto an anonymous voice that speaks the language of praise or blame. His discourse of recollection is ordered in the hierarchy of three genres beneath flyting; his rhetoric is still constrained by the imagined speech-acts of others (Martin 1993:136).

The first clear example comes in his meeting with his wife. The passage quotes an unnamed speaker's comment on seeing Andromakhe as a prize of war after the fall of Troy: "And sometime someone may say, 'Here is the wife of Hektor, he who excelled at fighting, of the horse-taming Trojans, when they fought about Ilion'" (6.459-61). The dramatic realism increases our sympathy for Andromakhe as it convinces us of Hektor's affection. But the actual content of the quote constitutes a snatch of praise-poetry concerning Hektor himself; in an oblique manner, Hektor is creating the exact format of his own reputation, like a poet writing his own epitaph (Martin 1993:135-36).

The reply of Achilles to Odysseus in Book 9 (9.307-429) has been the focus of a long debate about the "language of Achilles," originating with Adam Parry's argument that Achilles, "bound by the formulaic nature of his diction, has no language with which to express his disillusionment" and yet succeeds by "misusing the language he disposes of." M. D. Reeve persuasively challenged this view, pointing out that "neither an unanswerable question nor an impossible demand is by its nature a misuse of language." The approach that best illuminates Achilles' speech is to compare every expression in his reply with similar expressions elsewhere in the *Iliad*, treating deviation from that norm as the site of characterization. The resulting analysis operates at the level of discourse—the full stretch of a speech—rather than at the level of particles, verb moods, or metrical features, which would not be perceptible to a listening audience (Martin 1993:152-53, 159-60).

For this analysis, formulas are divided into two types: syntagmatic (regularly repeated phrase groups—Parry's original definition) and paradigmatic (single words in regular metrical positions). A close reading of Chart 1, the full formulaic breakdown of Achilles' speech (9.307-429), produces the following finding: almost all of the speech consists of formulas, either paradigmatic or syntagmatic. Achilles' speech is thus traditional, in terms of the *Iliad* itself. But the number of paradigmatic formulas far outweighs the number of syntagmatic: Achilles uses very small, unconnected bricks for his edifice, choosing rather to rely on the ready-made longer phrase only occasionally. Each choice of word in a given place in the line is one that can be paralleled elsewhere, but the effect is one of tone: one seems to hear a man searching laboriously for the right word at every turn (Martin 1993:165-66).

Achilles generally employs familiar formulas in new ways; at the same time, what seem like new and innovative uses can in fact be explained as reworkings of familiar expressions paralleled elsewhere within the poem. His idiosyncrasy in formula use consists in his use of syntagmatic expressions elsewhere used exclusively by gods in speeches, or by the narrator in diegesis. There is a cohesiveness to these "deviations" (Martin 1993:169).

The key innovation that characterizes Achilles' speech over all other heroic discourse is what will be demonstrated as the "expansion aesthetic": an ability to ornament by expansion. Only Achilles, for example, asks essentially the same rhetorical question three times in succession (9.337-39). What is not conventional is the expanded form he gives to this strategy; and it is this expansion aesthetic that explains the dozens of fine deviations from the norm that a close reading of the speech reveals. Ultimately, it is this expansion aesthetic that shows the motivations for Homer's own poetry (Martin 1993:204).

The phrase *kleos aphthiton* ("unwithering fame") at *Iliad* 9.413 is the most marked formula in Achilles' reply: it does not appear elsewhere in Homeric poetry, and its metrical shape has been shown through comparative work to presuppose a common Indo-European prototype representing within Greek "a fragment of Indo-European versification" (Nagy). Despite this heritage, some scholars have labeled the phrase a chance innovation, since it occurs only here in Homeric poetry and employs the adjective as a predicate, with *estai*. Yet the single attestation of the phrase is actually the best proof that *kleos aphthiton* is not an accident of composition. Instead, the phrase is used just once, at the most important moment in the most important speech of the *Iliad*, and I believe it is used knowingly, as an heirloom from the poet's word-hoard (Martin 1993:182-83).

We can detect similar thematic importance in other combinations that are foregrounded in Achilles' speech by virtue of their unique occurrence in the *Iliad*. Line 341 gives us an opportunity to see how a new combination can spring from older, more usual phrases. The adjective *agathos* frequently appears in the slot following the hephthemimeral caesura, from its use in the formulas *agathos Diomedes* and *boēn agathos*. Similarly, *anēr* appears frequently in its slot, yet is never qualified by an adjective defining "goodness." In this case, the new juxtaposition of two ordinary words implies an equally unparalleled way of looking at human behavior: Achilles breaks through to the abstract language of philosophical ethics (Martin 1993:182-83).

The phrase *thumalgea lōbēn* (9.387) involves another kind of uniqueness. The adjective *thumalgea* is regularly used with another noun, *kholos*, in a formula that has primary reference in the poem to Achilles' own anger. The other occurrences of the adjective are placed on either side of Achilles' speech in Book 9: Odysseus reminds him of Peleus' admonition to put aside the "soul-paining anger" (9.260), and Phoinix includes in his paraenetic tale the detail that Meleager nursed this kind of wrath (9.565)—which is equivalent to calling Achilles' own anger "soul-paining." When Achilles uses *thumalgea* with *lōbēn*, "disgrace," the poet is characterizing him as rhetorician once more. Achilles' displacement of the adjective is an implicit answer to his elders, which asserts that his anger is not without an equally grievous motivation in the treatment accorded him (Martin 1993:183-84).

Achilles' speech begins with the words *khrē men dē ton muthon apēlegeōs apoeipein* (9.309). The Greek word *khrē* is a strong expression of necessity and demands the attention of the listener. Furthermore, it sets off Achilles as a speaker distinct from the narrator, who never uses the word *khrē* in the *Iliad*. Achilles is concerned with the exigencies of life in a way that the omniscient Homer need not be. But besides being distinguished from the poet by his use of the word, Achilles alone among other speakers places *khrē* at the beginning of his discourse, and he alone combines it with the particle *men*. A rough paraphrase—"yes it is necessary, but"—shows that the combination transmits two messages at once. Something is crucial; at the same time, the speaker has other concerns: the anxious, serious tone thus created affects all the following words of the speaker. The combination *khrē men* occurs only one other time in the *Iliad*, at the point in Book 1 where Achilles replies to Athena (1.216-18) (Martin 1993:197-98).

Keeping in mind the conventional deployment of *khrē* phrases elsewhere in the poem—where they appear in contexts of battle advice or "talk about talk" (metalinguistic comment on another's speech)—Achilles' words are distinctive in a further way: he explicitly denies both topics. He will not fight, nor do his interlocutors need to speak to him about the decision. Achilles' denial of the effectiveness of speech goes beyond the usual expression which heroes use to silence one another for the moment; it becomes an abstract principle (Martin 1993:200-201).

Homer's *recusatio* at the threshold of the Catalogue of Ships—"I could not make a *muthos* or name the entirety, not even if I had ten tongues, ten mouths, an unbreakable voice, and my heart bronze within me, unless the Olympian Muses, daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus, should recall how many came up to Troy" (2.488-92)—has a precise parallel in Achilles' hyperbolic denial at 9.379: "Not even if Agamemnon gives ten and twenty times as much will he persuade me." Although their topics appear to be different, both Achilles and the narrator Homer speak in the same manner: both specify a condition using hyperbolic numbering, then deny the condition by adding a further one—Homer requires the Muses, not merely ten tongues; Achilles demands that Agamemnon pay back his insult, and no other payments will satisfy him. Finally, both Homer and Achilles use this trope to continue their speech-acts: Homer by listing the Panhellenic forces, Achilles by listing his own assets in Phthia. As with the occurrence of the unique formula *kleos aphthiton* in Achilles' speech, this particularly marked trope is restricted within the rhetoric of the *Iliad* and given to only one hero to speak—the performer who is most like Homer (Martin 1993:223-24).

This parallel furnishes the most important piece of evidence for the first of two conclusions arising from the analysis of Achilles' speech. First, the "language of Achilles" is none other than that of the monumental composer: Homer fully reveals all the possibilities of his own poetic craft only in the extended speech of Achilles. Homer could have expanded formulaic diction in the speeches of any other hero to produce discourse as complex, inward-looking, and pleonastic as Achilles', but he did not. The effect is to make Achilles sound like a poet, and the similarity arises because Homer, when he constructs Achilles by means of language, employs all his poetic resources and stretches the limits of his formulaic art to make the hero as large a figure as possible. In short, the monumental poem demands a monumental hero; the language of epic, pressed to provide speech for such a man, becomes the "language of Achilles" (Martin 1993:222-23).

The second conclusion is the converse: the rhetoric of Homer as narrator is that of a heroic performer in the role of Achilles. Achilles' performance situation is itself an image for the way in which the poem was composed. Homer's composition of the monumental *Iliad* starts from the need to outdistance previous epics. The rhetoric of Achilles—his heroic self-performance in an adversary relationship with the past and the present—is at the root of Homer's own composition-in-performance. If the language of Achilles is the undisguised voice and rhetoric of Homer himself, then the converse is equally true (Martin 1993:224-25, 230).

For the specific expansion types that underlie the rhetoric of Achilles' speech, see the comments on *Iliad* 9.310-314 and *Iliad* 9.315-316.

The poet seems to use it as a refrain; in context, the phrase both characterizes the tender relations between Achilles and Thetis, and Achilles and his companion, and introduces speech in such a way that we assume the following words are the candid outpourings of the speaker who is addressed (Martin 1993:209-10).

In the Conclusion to the book, the three occurrences of this formula have a further significance. Achilles is apostrophized eight times in the course of Book 16—more than any other figure in the poem at a comparable passage. If Homer puts on the role of his hero, making Achilles the "focalizer" of narration, this speech habit comes with it: Achilles, after all, is the one hero who most often addresses Patroklos in the course of the poem. Homer sees the death through the eyes of Achilles, his alter ego. In this regard, apostrophe is natural (Martin 1993:235-36).

For the broader argument that Achilles functions as focalizer of the narration, see the comment on *Iliad* 9.182-198.
